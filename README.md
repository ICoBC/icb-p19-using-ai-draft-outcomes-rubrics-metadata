# Using AI to Draft Outcomes, Rubrics and Metadata Responsibly

*A documented method for AI-assisted credential design.*

Course ID: ICB-P19 | Framework: ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework (ICSF), skill AIRC "Trustworthy AI in recognition", level L2 "Operate", stage A "Ability"

## About this course

AI tools can produce a first draft of learning outcomes, a rubric or a metadata description in seconds. They can also produce invented claims, wrong levels and biased wording in the same seconds. This course teaches a method that keeps the speed and controls the risk: good inputs, drafting with clear prompts, systematic checking, an edit log that shows your judgement, and strict protection of confidential and personal data.

**Who this course is for**

- L&D and corporate academy leads
- Programme and micro-credential leads
- Learning designers and teaching staff
- ICoBC Accredited Partners

**What you will be able to do**

At the ICSF level L2 "Operate", you use AI tools for defined credentialing tasks with human review and documentation. At the stage "Ability" you practise this on realistic tasks in a controlled setting.

**Learning outcomes**

1. Use AI tools to draft outcomes, rubrics and descriptions from source material.
2. Check drafts for accuracy, level, bias and invented claims.
3. Keep an edit log that shows human judgement.
4. Protect confidential and personal data when prompting.

**Workload**

4 hours notional learning, self-paced plus live. This file covers the self-paced part. Live or workshop elements appear as facilitator notes.

**Prerequisite**

ICB-F07 AI Literacy for Recognition Work.

**Assessment**

You produce one credential specification with AI support, plus your edit log. A person assesses both against the ICoBC P01 rubric and an AI use checklist. A short knowledge check in this file prepares you for that task. The file does not grade your specification.

**Badge**

"Using AI to Draft Outcomes, Rubrics and Metadata Responsibly (Ability, L2)", alignment code ICSF-AIRC-L2-A. Badge evidence: the specification and the edit log. Completing this course does not prove competency in a job. The badge records that you showed this capability on a defined task.

## Good inputs

### Why inputs decide quality

An AI tool writes from what you give it. If you give it a vague request, it fills the gaps with plausible text. In credential design, plausible text is risky: it can claim an assessment that does not exist, imply a level you have not set, or describe skills no one will teach.

Good inputs are the cheapest quality control you have. They shape the draft before you read a single word of it.

A good input pack has five parts:

1. **Source material.** The syllabus, job profile, competency model, assessment brief or subject-matter expert notes that the credential is based on. Use approved, current documents.
2. **Purpose and audience.** Who earns the credential, who reads it, and what decision it supports (for example hiring, promotion, credit or access to further study).
3. **Level and framework.** The target level in the framework you use, with the level descriptor text copied in. Do not ask the tool to "pitch it at level 5" without giving the descriptor.
4. **Constraints.** Word limits, required verbs or style rules, the number of outcomes, the metadata fields you must fill.
5. **Rules for the tool.** For example: "Use only the source material. If something is missing, write MISSING instead of guessing."

The last rule matters most. It turns silent invention into a visible gap that you can fix.

### Cleaning and preparing source material

Before you paste anything into a tool, prepare it.

- **Choose the right version.** An out-of-date syllabus produces an out-of-date credential.
- **Remove what the tool does not need.** Names of learners, staff and clients, contract details, internal pricing and anything marked confidential stay out. Module 5 covers this in detail.
- **Mark the source.** Give each source a short label, such as S1, S2, S3. Ask the tool to cite these labels next to each statement. This makes checking much faster in Module 3.
- **Separate facts from wishes.** If the programme team would like the credential to cover a topic that the teaching does not cover, say so. A draft should describe what is taught and assessed.

### Writing the task instruction

State the task in one sentence, then add the format.

> Task: Draft five learning outcomes for the credential described in S1 and S2. Format: one sentence each, starting with an observable verb, no more than 25 words. After each outcome, add the source label that supports it. If no source supports an outcome, do not write it.

Notice what this instruction does. It limits the task, fixes the format, forces a source link and gives the tool a way to say "no".

**Worked example: A short course at a training provider**

Maria is a programme lead at a vocational training provider. She wants to turn a two-day "Introduction to workplace first aid" course into a micro-credential. She has three approved documents: the course outline (S1), the assessment checklist used by trainers (S2) and the national level descriptors she must align to (S3).

She prepares her input pack:

- Sources: S1, S2, S3, with learner names removed from the checklist.
- Purpose: a credential for people who act as first aiders in small workplaces. Employers read it to confirm that a person completed a taught and assessed course.
- Level: she pastes the descriptor text from S3 for the target level.
- Constraints: five outcomes, one sentence each, observable verbs.
- Rule: use only S1 to S3 and write MISSING if information is absent.

Her prompt asks for outcomes with source labels. The first draft returns five outcomes, each with a label, and one line reading "MISSING: duration of validity of the credential". That gap is real: the provider has not yet decided it. Maria now knows she needs a decision, instead of finding an invented "valid for three years" in the published text.

**Practice activity: Build an input pack**

Choose a real or realistic credential idea from your own work. If you have none, use this scenario: a corporate academy in a logistics company wants a micro-credential for team leaders on "Giving feedback in the workplace".

1. List the sources you would use and give each a label (S1, S2, S3).
2. Write the purpose and audience in two sentences.
3. Name the framework and level, and note which descriptor text you would paste in.
4. List three constraints.
5. Write one rule that makes the tool show gaps.
6. Write the one-sentence task instruction.

Check your pack against the five parts above. Keep it: you reuse it in Module 2.

## Drafting

### Prompting for three kinds of output

AI tools help most when the task is narrow. In credential design you draft three kinds of text again and again: outcomes, rubrics and descriptions (including metadata fields). Each needs its own instruction pattern.

**Outcomes.** Ask for observable, assessable statements. Fix the verb style and the count. Ask the tool to say which source supports each one. Ask it to avoid vague verbs such as "understand" or "be aware of", unless you define how they will be shown.

**Rubrics.** A rubric needs criteria, performance levels and descriptors that differ clearly from level to level. Give the tool the outcomes, the assessment task and the number of levels. Ask for descriptors that describe observable evidence and avoid words that only repeat each other ("good", "very good", "excellent").

**Descriptions and metadata.** Credential descriptions often have fields such as title, summary, target audience, learning outcomes, assessment method, level, workload and validity. Give the tool the exact list of fields and any length limit. Ask it to leave a field as MISSING if the sources do not provide the content. Never let it fill in fields such as workload, issuer name or validity from general knowledge.

### Working in small steps

One long prompt that asks for everything produces a draft that is hard to check. Work in small steps instead.

1. Ask for outcomes only. Review them.
2. Give the approved outcomes back and ask for a rubric. Review it.
3. Give the approved outcomes and rubric back and ask for the description and metadata. Review them.

Each step starts from text that a human has approved. Errors do not travel from one step to the next.

You can also ask for two or three alternative versions of one outcome and choose the best. Choosing and combining is a clear sign of human judgement, and you will record it in Module 4.

### Useful prompt patterns

Use these patterns as starting points. Adapt them to your sources.

- **Role and limit:** "You assist a learning designer. Use only the sources S1 to S3. Do not add facts."
- **Format:** "Return a table with the columns: outcome, verb, source label."
- **Gap rule:** "If the sources do not support a statement, write MISSING and explain what is needed."
- **Review request:** "List any statement in your draft that might not be supported by the sources." This does not replace your own check, but it can point you to places to look.
- **Revision:** "Revise outcome 3 to be assessable by a practical task. Keep the meaning. Show the changes."

Save the prompts you use. Module 4 explains why.

**Worked example: A rubric draft in a corporate academy**

Tomas is a learning designer in the corporate academy of a logistics company. The academy offers a micro-credential "Giving feedback in the workplace" for team leaders. The assessment task is a recorded 10-minute feedback conversation, assessed by a trained observer.

Tomas has approved outcomes from step 1. For step 2, he sends the outcomes, the task description and this instruction: "Draft a rubric with four criteria and three performance levels: Not yet, Meets, Exceeds. Describe observable behaviour in the recording. Avoid repeating the same adjective across levels. Use only the task description. Write MISSING where the sources do not say how evidence is observed."

The tool returns a rubric. One criterion, "Agrees next steps", has the descriptor for "Exceeds": "Excellent agreement of next steps." Tomas sees that this repeats the criterion name and describes nothing observable. He asks for a revision: "Rewrite the Exceeds descriptor for 'Agrees next steps' so that an observer can check it in the recording." The new version reads: "Leader and colleague agree at least one specific next step, with a date, and the leader summarises it aloud." Tomas accepts this version and notes the change.

**Practice activity: Draft in steps**

Use the input pack you built in Module 1 (or the logistics scenario).

1. Using an AI tool that your organisation permits, ask for three to five outcomes. Include the source labels and the MISSING rule. Use only non-confidential, approved material.
2. Choose, edit or reject each outcome. Write one line per outcome saying why.
3. Give the approved outcomes back and ask for a rubric with three performance levels.
4. Identify two descriptors that are vague or repeat each other. Ask for a revision of each.
5. Save all prompts and drafts. You need them for Modules 3 and 4.

If you have no access to an AI tool, ask a colleague to provide a draft from your input pack, or use the sample draft in the facilitator notes.

## Checking

### What to check and why

A fluent draft looks finished. That is the danger. Fluency tells you nothing about accuracy. Checking is the step where human judgement protects the credential and the people who rely on it.

Check every AI-assisted draft against four questions: accuracy, level, bias and invented claims. Work through them in order, with the sources next to the draft.

**Accuracy.** Does each statement match the approved sources? Use the source labels. For each statement, find the passage that supports it. If you cannot find one, mark the statement as unsupported. Also check names, dates, numbers, titles of standards and references to laws or frameworks. Tools can produce references that look right and do not exist.

**Level.** Does the wording match the level descriptor you chose? Compare verbs and scope. Words such as "design", "evaluate" and "lead" suggest higher levels of autonomy and complexity than "describe", "follow" and "assist". Check the outcome, the rubric descriptors and the assessment task together: if the outcome asks for evaluation and the task only asks for recall, the credential has a level problem.

**Bias.** Look at language and assumptions. Do descriptors favour one style of communication, accent, cultural norm or physical ability without a reason that relates to the outcome? Are examples built around one kind of person or workplace? Are terms such as "native speaker", "young", "energetic" or "strong personality" present? Check whether a competent person with a disability or a different background could meet the outcome, and ask whether the wording would block them for a reason unrelated to the competence.

**Invented claims.** This covers anything the draft says that the sources do not: an assessment that does not take place, a partnership, an accreditation, a level of recognition, a pass rate, a validity period, a workload figure. Invented claims in a credential description can mislead learners and employers. Treat every claim about recognition, quality assurance or regulation with special care.

### A practical checking routine

Use the same routine each time so that nothing is skipped.

1. Print or display the draft and the sources side by side.
2. Go through the draft line by line. Mark each line: supported, partly supported, unsupported.
3. For each outcome, compare the verb and scope with the level descriptor.
4. Read the whole text once for bias, using a short list of questions.
5. Search for numbers, names and recognition claims. Verify each one in an official source.
6. Decide for each issue: accept, edit, reject or ask for a new draft. Record the decision (Module 4).

Do not rely on a second AI tool to check the first. It can help you find possible issues, but it can share the same blind spots, and it cannot confirm facts from sources it has not seen. The decision remains yours.

A word on detection tools: tools that claim to detect AI-written text are not a reliable basis for decisions. Do not use them to judge whether a colleague or a learner has used AI. Judge the quality and accuracy of the work and follow your organisation's published rules on AI use.

**Worked example: Checking a draft in a university setting**

Dr Amara Okafor is a programme lead at a university. She asks an AI tool to draft a description for a micro-credential "Research data management basics" from a course outline (S1) and the university's level descriptors (S2). The draft says:

## Documenting

### Why an edit log

If a credential specification is challenged, you need to show how it was made. Who decided what? Which parts came from a tool? What did a person change, and why? An edit log answers these questions. It also shows that human judgement shaped the result, which is the core of responsible AI use at this level.

An edit log is a short, structured record. It is not a diary and it is not a copy of the whole chat. It captures decisions.

### What to record

For each AI-assisted piece of work, record:

- **Date and person.** Who worked on this and when.
- **Tool and settings.** The type of tool or model and its version if known, and relevant settings. Name the tool as your organisation describes it internally.
- **Task and prompt.** The prompt, or a reference to a saved prompt file. Note which sources were provided and their labels.
- **Output reference.** Where the raw output is stored, so the draft can be traced.
- **Issues found.** What you found in the checks: accuracy, level, bias or invented claims.
- **Decision.** Accept, edit, reject or regenerate.
- **Reason.** A short sentence explaining why. This is the evidence of judgement.
- **Final text reference.** Where the approved wording is stored.

A simple table works well:

| Date | Person | Element | Prompt ref | AI output summary | Issue found | Decision | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 Nov 2026 | A. Okafor | Outcome 2 | P-03 | "Lead institutional data policy" | Level too high (S2) | Edited | Descriptor for this level is guided work |

The reason column is the most important. "Edited" alone shows that something changed. "Edited: level too high against S2" shows judgement.

### Keeping the log honest and useful

- **Log as you go.** Entries written weeks later are incomplete and less credible.
- **Log rejections.** Rejected drafts show that you did not accept output automatically.
- **Keep prompts and raw outputs.** Store them with the log, protecting any data in them according to your policy. Do not store confidential material in places where it can be seen by people without access.
- **Write plainly.** Another person should understand each entry without asking you.
- **Disclose AI use.** Add a short statement to the specification: which parts were AI-assisted, which tool type was used, who reviewed, and where the log is kept. Follow your organisation's rules on disclosure.

### Linking the log to quality

The log supports the VRITE pillars of Rigor and Trustworthiness. Rigor appears in the structured checks. Trustworthiness appears when a reviewer, an auditor or a partner can follow the trail from prompt to decision.

Keep the log in a form that can be shared as evidence. For the badge, you submit the specification and the edit log together. An assessor will look for three things: that checks were done, that decisions have reasons, and that a person, not the tool, made the final choices.

An AI use checklist helps you stay consistent. A one-page version appears in the Assessment and badge section. Use it as you work, not only at the end.

**Worked example: An edit log in a corporate academy**

Tomas, the learning designer from Module 2, records his work on the feedback rubric.

| Date | Person | Element | Prompt ref | AI output summary | Issue found | Decision | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 Nov 2026 | T. Weber | Rubric criterion 3, Exceeds | P-07 | "Excellent agreement of next steps" | Not observable; repeats criterion name | Regenerated, then edited | Observer cannot check "excellent"; new text names date and spoken summary |
| 5 Nov 2026 | T. Weber | Rubric criterion 1, Meets | P-07 | "Opens in a respectful manner" | Vague; possible cultural bias | Edited | Replaced with "states purpose of conversation within first minute" (S2) |
| 5 Nov 2026 | T. Weber | Metadata: validity | P-08 | "Valid for two years" | Not in any source | Rejected | Issuer has not decided; marked MISSING and sent to academy director |

## Data protection

### What not to put into a prompt

Anything you type into an AI tool may be stored, processed or reviewed in ways you do not control, depending on the tool and its settings. Treat prompts as a form of sharing. Before you paste anything in, ask: am I allowed to share this with this tool?

Three groups of data need special care.

**Personal data.** Any information about an identifiable person: names, email addresses, learner or employee numbers, photographs, assessment results, feedback comments, health information, performance reviews. Credential design rarely needs this data. If you want the tool to improve a rubric, you can describe a case in general terms without the person's identity.

**Confidential organisational data.** Internal strategy, client names and contract terms, unpublished products, pricing, security procedures, proprietary methods, and documents marked confidential. A corporate academy that pastes a client's process manual into a public tool may breach contract terms.

**Protected or licensed content.** Textbooks, standards texts, proprietary frameworks and third-party materials may be restricted by licence. Pasting long extracts into a tool may not be permitted. Check the licence or ask the rights holder.

### Practical protections

- **Use approved tools.** Use only tools your organisation has approved for the type of data involved. Ask your data protection officer or IT team which tools and settings are allowed.
- **Minimise.** Share the least information that gets the job done. Summarise instead of pasting full documents where possible.
- **Remove and replace.** Remove names and identifiers. Use roles such as "Learner A" or "the team leader" instead.
- **Check settings.** Find out whether the tool stores prompts, uses them for training, or allows you to opt out. If you do not know, assume the least protective case.
- **Separate environments.** Keep prompts with sensitive content out of shared chat histories, shared accounts and public channels.
- **Report mistakes quickly.** If you paste something you should not have, tell the responsible person at once. Quick action limits harm and is usually required by data protection rules.

### The legal frame

In many countries, including those under the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), you need a lawful basis to process personal data, you must limit it to what is necessary, and you must protect it. Sharing personal data with a tool operated by another organisation is a form of processing. Your organisation may need an agreement with the provider and may need to carry out an impact assessment. These are questions for your data protection officer. This course does not give legal advice.

The EU AI Act adds duties for certain AI uses, particularly where AI is used to make or support decisions about people. As noted in Module 3, check the final published text of the AI Omnibus for the application dates. ### Learners and integrity

If your credential allows learners to use AI in their own work, the specification should say so. State what is permitted, what must be disclosed, and how the assessor judges authenticity. Do not rely on detection tools for such decisions, because they are not reliable. Design the assessment so that evidence of the learner's own thinking is visible: a viva, a process log, a live demonstration or a reflective commentary can all help.

**Worked example: Preparing source material at a training provider**

Fatima is a quality lead at a training provider. She wants an AI tool to help improve the rubric for a "customer service basics" credential. Her raw material is an assessor report with the following content: learner names, scores, assessor comments such as "Jonas struggled with his hearing aid in the role-play" and the name of a retail client whose till process was used.

Fatima follows these steps:

1. **Decide what the task needs.** She needs examples of how descriptors worked in practice. She does not need names, health details or client information.
2. **Remove and replace.** She replaces names with "Learner A", "Learner B". She deletes the comment about the hearing aid completely, because it is health information and is not needed. She replaces the client name with "a retail client".
3. **Use an approved tool.** She uses the tool approved by her provider for internal documents and checks that prompt storage is covered by the provider's agreement.
4. **Record it.** In her edit log, she notes what was removed and why.
5. **Keep the original safe.** The original report stays in the provider's secure system.

The tool now receives only general information about how descriptors performed.

### Quiz: Data protection

**Question 1.** Which item should never be pasted into a prompt for a drafting task?

[( )] The published title of the credential
[( )] The approved level descriptor from the framework
[(X)] An assessor comment that names a learner and mentions a health condition
[( )] A list of the metadata fields the credential must contain

****
Names combined with health information are personal data and, in the case of health, usually special category data. Drafting tasks do not need it.
****

**Question 2.** You have accidentally pasted a client's confidential pricing document into a tool. What is the best next step?

[( )] Delete the chat and say nothing
[( )] Wait to see whether the output contains anything sensitive
[(X)] Tell the responsible person, for example your data protection officer or line manager, without delay
[( )] Paste the same document again to compare results

****
Quick reporting limits harm and is usually required by policy or law. Deleting the chat does not remove copies that may have been stored.
****

**Question 3.** What does "minimise" mean when preparing a prompt?

[( )] Use the shortest possible task instruction
[(X)] Share only the information that the task needs
[( )] Remove all source labels
[( )] Use a small number of prompts

****
Minimisation means limiting the data you share to what is necessary. It is also a core principle of data protection law in many countries.
****

**Question 4.** A training provider wants to use real learner assessment results to improve a rubric. Which approach is most appropriate?

[( )] Paste the full results table with names so the tool can see the patterns
[( )] Paste the results with only surnames removed
[(X)] Use an approved tool with anonymised, aggregated information, and ask the data protection officer first
[( )] Use a free public tool because the data is only grades

****
Anonymised or aggregated information reduces risk, and an approved tool with checked terms is needed. The data protection officer can confirm the legal basis and any further steps.
****

## Assessment and badge

### The task

You produce one credential specification with AI support, and the edit log that shows how you made it. A person assesses both against the ICoBC P01 rubric and the AI use checklist. This file does not grade your work.

**Choose a context.** Use a real credential idea from your work, or one of the following:

- *University, college or training provider:* a micro-credential "Research data management basics" for postgraduate students.
- *Corporate academy or HR department:* a micro-credential "Giving feedback in the workplace" for team leaders.

**Your specification contains:**

1. Title and a summary of no more than 100 words.
2. Target audience and purpose.
3. Three to five learning outcomes, each linked to a source label.
4. The assessment method and a rubric with at least three criteria and three performance levels.
5. Level, with the framework descriptor you aligned to.
6. Workload and validity, or MISSING with a named decision-maker.
7. An AI use statement and a data protection statement (what was shared, what was removed, which tool type).

**Your edit log contains:**

- At least eight entries across outcomes, rubric and metadata.
- At least two accepted, two edited and one rejected item.
- A reason for each decision that refers to a source, a descriptor or a rule.
- Notes on bias and invented-claim checks.
- References to saved prompts and outputs.

**Rules:**

- Do not include real personal data or confidential material in the files you submit.
- Declare which tool type you used, as your organisation describes it.
- Follow your organisation's policy on AI use.

Assessors: use the P01 rubric for the specification and the AI use checklist for the process. Do not use AI detection tools to judge submissions. Judge accuracy, level, bias, invented claims, quality of reasons in the log and data protection. Give written feedback on each rubric row. Allow one resubmission after feedback.

### Assessment rubric

This rubric covers the evidence for the badge. The ICoBC P01 rubric for the credential specification applies in full. <!-- VERIFY: align criteria and wording with the current published P01 rubric before release -->

| Criterion | Not yet | Meets | Exceeds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inputs and prompts (Outcome 1) | Prompts have no sources, level or constraints | Prompts include labelled sources, level descriptor, constraints and a gap rule | Prompts are built in steps, reuse approved text and show clear improvement across rounds |
| Quality of drafted outcomes, rubric and description (Outcome 1) | Outcomes are vague or not assessable; rubric levels are not distinct | Outcomes are observable and linked to sources; rubric descriptors differ by observable evidence | Outcomes, rubric and task are tightly aligned; descriptors are precise and usable by a new assessor |
| Checking for accuracy, level, bias and invented claims (Outcome 2) | Checks are missing or unsupported claims remain | All four checks are visible; unsupported or invented claims are removed or resolved | Checks find subtle issues; each resolved issue is traceable to a source or descriptor |
| Edit log and human judgement (Outcome 3) | Log is missing, incomplete or lists changes without reasons | At least eight entries with accepted, edited and rejected items and a reason for each | Reasons are specific and cite sources; log shows escalation of open decisions to the right person |
| Data protection (Outcome 4) | Personal or confidential data appears in prompts or files | No personal or confidential data; statement says what was removed and which tool type was used | Statement is precise; minimisation and approved-tool use are clearly shown and risks are named |
| Transparency | No AI use statement | AI use statement names assisted parts, tool type, reviewer and log location | Statement is clear to a lay reader and consistent with the log |

### Pass rule

You pass when the assessor rates every criterion at "Meets" or above and no data protection issue remains. A rating of "Not yet" on any criterion leads to feedback and one resubmission. Data protection is a gate: personal or confidential data in the submitted files means the submission is returned for correction before it is assessed further.

### Knowledge check

This check prepares you for the assessed task. Answer all ten questions.

**Question 1.** You prepare an input pack for a drafting task. Which combination is strongest?

[( )] A short description of the topic and a request for "five great outcomes"
[(X)] Approved, labelled sources, the level descriptor, constraints on format and a rule to write MISSING when sources are silent
[( )] A competitor's outcomes and a request to rephrase them
[( )] The complete programme folder including learner records

****
Strong inputs are approved, labelled and limited, and they tell the tool how to show gaps. This addresses Outcome 1.
****

**Question 2.** Why does it help to draft outcomes, then the rubric, then the description, in separate steps?

[( )] Tools cannot handle more than one task per day
[(X)] Each step builds on text a person has approved, so errors do not carry forward
[( )] It removes the need for checking
[( )] It makes the edit log shorter

****
Separate steps create review points. This addresses Outcome 1.
****

**Question 3.** A rubric descriptor reads "Very good teamwork." What should you do?

[( )] Keep it, because it is positive and easy to read
[( )] Replace it with "Excellent teamwork" for the higher level
[(X)] Rewrite it as observable evidence, for example what the learner says or does in the task
[( )] Delete the criterion

****
Rubric descriptors need observable evidence so that assessors can judge consistently. This addresses Outcome 1.
****

**Question 4.** An AI draft states that the credential "is endorsed by the sector skills council." No source says so. What is the correct action?

[( )] Keep it, because sector endorsement is common
[( )] Ask the tool to confirm the endorsement
[(X)] Remove it unless an official source confirms it, and log the decision with a reason
[( )] Move it to a footnote

****
This is an invented claim. Only the issuer's verified information can support recognition claims. This addresses Outcomes 2 and 3.
****

**Question 5.** An outcome uses the verb "design" but the level descriptor describes carrying out defined tasks under guidance. Which check detects the problem?

[( )] The accuracy check of dates
[(X)] The level check, which compares verbs and scope with the descriptor
[( )] The formatting check
[( )] The metadata completeness check

****
Verbs and scope signal the level of autonomy and complexity. This addresses Outcome 2.
****

**Question 6.** Which descriptor is most likely to disadvantage some learners for reasons unrelated to the outcome?

[( )] "Explains the process in clear language, using examples"
[( )] "Records the agreed actions in writing"
[(X)] "Speaks fluently and confidently without notes"
[( )] "Asks questions to check understanding"

****
Fluency and confidence without notes can disadvantage people for reasons unrelated to the competence, unless it is the outcome itself. Descriptors should focus on observable evidence that matters for the outcome. This addresses Outcome 2.
****

**Question 7.** A manager suggests using an AI detection tool to decide whether a learner's submission was AI-written, and to fail the learner on that basis. What is the best response?

[( )] Agree, if the tool reports a high probability
[( )] Agree, if two tools give the same result
[(X)] Decline: detection tools are not a reliable basis for decisions; follow the published assessment rules and judge the evidence of the learner's own work
[( )] Ask the learner to use a different AI tool

****
Detection tools are unreliable. Decisions should rest on evidence and agreed rules. This addresses Outcome 2 and the topic of generative AI and assessment integrity.
****

**Question 8.** Which edit log entry is the strongest evidence of human judgement?

[( )] "Edited outcome 2."
[( )] "Tool wording was weak."
[(X)] "Outcome 2 edited: verb 'evaluate' exceeds level descriptor in S2; replaced with 'describe' and linked to S1 section 3."
[( )] "Accepted all outcomes after reading."

****
A strong entry names the issue, the source and the action. This addresses Outcome 3.
****

**Question 9.** Why should rejected AI suggestions appear in the edit log?

[( )] Rejected suggestions are not relevant
[(X)] They show that output was not accepted automatically and that checks were applied
[( )] They increase the word count of the log
[( )] They replace the need for an AI use statement

****
Rejections are evidence of checking and decision-making. This addresses Outcome 3.
****

**Question 10.** You hold an assessor report containing learner names, scores and a health remark, and want help to improve a rubric. What should you do first?

[( )] Paste the report into any available tool and ask it to ignore personal details
[( )] Remove only the health remark and paste the rest
[(X)] Decide what the task needs, remove or replace personal and confidential data, use an approved tool, and note the removals in your log
[( )] Ask the tool for permission to process the data

****
Minimise, remove or replace, use approved tools and document. Ask your data protection officer if unsure. This addresses Outcome 4.
****
